# Multi-omic factor analysis of sex differences in Parkinson's disease in PPMI and PDBP

Draft, 2026-11-19. PDBP values are from the rerun with library-size normalization (results in `results/mofa/main_noplasma/` and `results/mofa/single_group/`; pre-fix values in `results/mofa/pre_normalization_fix/`). References were retrieved from PubMed and checked with `docs/references/verify_refs.py` (record, DOI and abstract quote); see `docs/references/references_mofa.verified.csv`.

## Summary

We asked whether blood transcriptomic and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) proteomic variation in Parkinson's disease (PD) differs between women and men. Multi-omics factor analysis (MOFA) was trained on baseline whole-blood RNA-seq and CSF proteomics from the Parkinson's Progression Markers Initiative (PPMI; 1,329 participants), with sex as the grouping variable, and the learned factors were projected into the Parkinson's Disease Biomarkers Program (PDBP; 1,248 participants). Nine of the ten factors were reproduced in at least nine of ten subsamples of PPMI, and no factor was active in only one sex. One factor (Factor 7) separated PD from controls in both cohorts (log-odds per SD, PPMI −0.19, q = 0.001; PDBP −0.44, p < 0.001), with similar effects in women and men, and the association held after adjustment for library size and genes detected. A second factor met the replication rule but appears to be technical, and its PD association in PPMI did not survive that adjustment. No factor's association with diagnosis, motor severity or cognition differed by sex in a way that replicated. A sensitivity model without sex grouping, which keeps average sex differences, gave the same result. Within the precision of these cohorts, the dominant shared axes of blood and CSF variation in early PD do not differ by sex.

## Introduction

Women and men differ in PD incidence, age at onset and clinical presentation (Wooten et al., 2004; Haaxma et al., 2007). Transcriptomic sex differences in PD have been described mainly in post-mortem brain (López-Cerdán et al., 2022; Tranchevent et al., 2023). Whether they have a counterpart in blood and cerebrospinal fluid of living patients is less clear.

Multi-omics factor analysis decomposes several data types measured on the same people into a small number of latent factors (Argelaguet et al., 2018, 2020). Each factor is a pattern of covariation shared across features and, possibly, across data types. In its multi-group form, MOFA estimates the same feature weights for all groups but lets each group have its own factor activity, so a factor can be present in one group and absent in another. Grouping participants by sex therefore asks a specific question: are the major axes of multi-omic variation shared by women and men, or are some present in one sex only?

We applied multi-group MOFA to baseline whole-blood RNA-seq and CSF proteomics in PPMI and tested the factors in an independent cohort, PDBP. We then asked whether any factor related to PD differently in women and men, using interaction models in both cohorts and a second MOFA design that keeps average sex differences.

## Methods

### Cohorts and data

Data came from the Accelerating Medicines Partnership for Parkinson's Disease (AMP-PD) release 2023_v4 (BigQuery dataset `2023_v4release_1027`). We used the baseline visit (`visit_month` = 0) of two cohorts: PPMI (Parkinson Progression Marker Initiative, 2011) as the discovery cohort and PDBP as the validation cohort. Whole-blood RNA-seq for both cohorts was generated by AMP-PD (Craig et al., 2021).

The discovery set comprised 1,329 PPMI participants with recorded sex and at least one omic view: 1,308 with whole-blood RNA-seq and 354 with CSF proteomics. By diagnosis, it included 753 participants with PD, 555 controls and 21 with another diagnosis; 613 were women and 716 were men. The validation set comprised 1,248 PDBP participants with whole-blood RNA-seq, 107 of whom also had CSF proteomics. It included 767 participants with PD and 481 controls; 554 were women and 694 were men. In PDBP, diagnosis was taken from `diagnosis_at_baseline`, with "Parkinson's Disease" coded as PD and "No PD Nor Other Neurological Disorder" as control.

Clinical variables were age at baseline, MDS-UPDRS Part III summary score and MoCA total score. PPMI recorded more than one baseline MDS-UPDRS Part III exam for 162 participants, all of whom were on PD medication. For each of them we kept one exam: the off-medication exam when available (137 participants), otherwise the exam with no recorded medication state (19), otherwise the on-medication exam (6).

### RNA-seq processing

Gene counts were processed separately in each cohort. Within each cohort, genes with at least 10 counts in at least 20% of samples were used to estimate DESeq2 median-of-ratios size factors (PyDESeq2). Counts were divided by the size factors and log-transformed as log(x + 1). In PPMI, the 5,000 genes with the highest variance were kept and centered per gene. Sequencing plate was then removed with limma `removeBatchEffect`, with sex and diagnosis in the design so that their effects were preserved. PDBP was normalized and batch-corrected the same way within PDBP, and its genes were restricted to the 5,000 genes of the PPMI model. No PDBP data were used to select genes.

### CSF and plasma proteomics processing

AMP-PD provides batch-corrected protein abundance matrices (PDIA) for CSF and plasma. In these matrices, exact zeros mark proteins that were not detected. Kept as values, a zero becomes an outlier about 17 log2 units below a typical protein after log transformation. We therefore set zeros to missing. In PPMI, samples with more than 50% zeros were removed first, then proteins with more than 30% zeros among the remaining samples. Values were log2(x + 1) transformed and each sample was shifted to the common median. PDBP received the same sample filter and transformation. Its proteins were those of the PPMI model.

For CSF, no samples or proteins exceeded the thresholds, and all 249 proteins were kept. For plasma, 2 of 167 PPMI samples and 43 of 202 proteins were removed, leaving 159 proteins. Plasma was excluded from the main model (see Results). The three-view model including plasma was kept as a sensitivity analysis.

### Multi-group MOFA

The main model was fitted with mofapy2 0.7.5 on two views, RNA-seq (5,000 genes) and CSF proteomics (249 proteins), with participants grouped by sex. Each view used a Gaussian likelihood. Features were centered within each group and each view was scaled to unit variance. The model had 10 factors, automatic relevance determination priors on factors and weights, and a spike-and-slab prior on weights. All participants with at least one view were included; MOFA treats missing views as missing data rather than excluding the participant. Training used the slow convergence criterion with a limit of 1,000 iterations; the main model converged after 220 iterations.

mofapy2 initializes factors from a principal component analysis of the data, so the random seed only perturbs a randomized PCA solver. Five seeds of the three-view model converged after 220 iterations to the same solution (final ELBOs within 0.07 of −5,017,117.7; every factor matched across seeds with |r| = 1.0). Seed restarts therefore say little about stability, and we assessed stability by subsampling instead.

### Variance explained and projection into PDBP

Variance explained (R²) was computed per view, per group and per factor with the mofapy2 definition, R²ₖ = 1 − Σ(Y − zₖwₖᵀ)² / ΣY², over observed entries only. Values from different views or groups have different denominators and were not summed.

PDBP participants were projected into the PPMI factor space with the trained weights held fixed. PDBP data were first transformed as MOFA transforms training data: each feature was centered within each sex and each view scaled to unit variance. For each participant, the factor scores were the posterior mean z = (WₒᵀTₒWₒ + A)⁻¹WₒᵀTₒyₒ, computed over that participant's observed features. Here T is the per-feature noise precision and A the factor prior precision of the participant's sex, both read from the trained model. Missing views and missing values were left out of the calculation rather than filled with zeros. PDBP R² was computed with the same formula as PPMI.

### Stability

Stability was assessed by retraining the model on 10 subsets of 80% of PPMI participants, drawn within each sex. For each factor of the full model, we found the subset factor with the highest absolute Pearson correlation of factor scores across shared participants. A factor was called stable if this correlation was at least 0.8 in at least 90% of subsets. For matched factors we also report the correlation of weights in each view and the overlap (Jaccard index) of the top 5% of features by absolute weight.

### Clinical association and interaction models

Each cohort's factor scores were standardized, and MDS-UPDRS Part III and MoCA were standardized within the cohort's PD cases. Metadata were reduced to one row per participant. For each factor we fitted:

- factor ~ PD × male + age, in cases and controls, and the PD effect within each sex (factor ~ PD + age);
- factor ~ score × male + age, and the within-sex score effect, for MDS-UPDRS Part III and MoCA in PD cases only, so that severity is not confounded with diagnosis;
- logit(PD) ~ factor + age + male;
- score ~ factor + age + male, in PD cases.

PPMI participants with a diagnosis other than PD or control were excluded from these models. p-values were adjusted across the 10 factors by the Benjamini-Hochberg method, separately for each cohort, model and term. A result was considered replicated when PPMI q < 0.05, PDBP p < 0.05 and the two estimates had the same sign. Unadjusted associations with plate, sex, age, diagnosis and the clinical scores (Pearson correlation or one-way ANOVA) were used only to describe factors.

### Pathway enrichment

For the replicated PD factor (Factor 7) and the age factor (Factor 10), all RNA-seq genes were ranked by their weight and tested with GSEA preranked (gseapy; 1,000 permutations; gene sets of 15-500 genes after matching) against MSigDB 2024.1 Hallmark, Reactome and GO Biological Process. Ensembl IDs were mapped to gene symbols with MyGene.info; 4,324 of the 5,000 genes mapped, and the rest were mostly unannotated or non-coding transcripts. The full weight vector was used rather than a top-gene cut-off, because the overlap of top-weighted genes across subsamples was only moderate. The universe is the model's 5,000 most variable genes, so the results describe which pathways a factor separates among those genes, not genome-wide enrichment. A positive normalized enrichment score (NES) means higher expression in participants with higher factor scores.

As a secondary view, the 50 genes with the largest positive and the 50 with the largest negative weights that map to a STRING protein (STRING 12.5) were taken for each factor. We retrieved high-confidence interactions among them (combined score ≥ 0.7) and ran STRING functional enrichment for each direction, with the model's own genes as the background (2,810 of 4,315 symbols mapped to STRING). Immunoglobulin segments, most lncRNAs and pseudogenes have no STRING protein and cannot appear in these networks.

### Sensitivity analyses

Grouping by sex centers every feature within each sex before training, which removes average sex differences by design. To test whether this hid a sex-specific PD signal, we trained a single-group model on the same inputs without sex grouping. Sex-chromosome genes were kept, consistent with the decision not to regress out biological variables before MOFA. Factors were matched between the two models by correlation of factor scores, and the single-group model was analyzed with the same projection, stability and clinical models as the main model.

The three-view model, which adds plasma proteomics as a third view, was analyzed the same way.

## Results

### Plasma proteomics was dominated by non-detects and was excluded

Before zeros were set to missing, they accounted for 63% of the processed plasma view's variance in PPMI and 71% in PDBP. Zeros made up 14.4% of plasma values in PPMI and 7.9% in PDBP, were not associated with plate, and were concentrated in a subset of proteins: 45 proteins had more than 30% zeros in PPMI. The first principal component of plasma was driven by these zero-rich proteins.

After zeros were set to missing, plasma had structure of its own (first principal component, 16% of variance), but MOFA did not capture it. In the three-view model, no factor explained more than 0.7% of plasma variance in PPMI, plasma R² totalled 1.2-1.5%, and the PDBP plasma R² was below zero. None of the first five plasma principal components was associated with age, diagnosis, sex, plate or any MOFA factor (all −log10 p ≤ 2.4). We therefore excluded plasma from the main model. Removing it changed per-factor R² in RNA-seq and CSF by at most 0.01 percentage points.

CSF had the same artifact at lower frequency. Zeros were only 0.02% of CSF values, but as outliers they contributed 16% of the CSF view's variance in PPMI. Setting them to missing raised the variance on the first CSF principal component from 23% to 27%.

### Ten factors described shared variation in women and men

The main model explained 66.4% of RNA-seq variance in women and 69.9% in men, and 24.3% and 27.5% of CSF variance. Two factors carried most of this. Factor 1 explained 56.9% (women) and 61.7% (men) of RNA-seq variance and had no clinical association. It may reflect blood cell composition, which PD is known to alter in whole blood (Craig et al., 2021) but which we have not tested directly. Factor 1 scores rose with the number of genes detected per sample (Spearman ρ = 0.82 in PPMI, 0.59 in PDBP). Factor 2 explained 23.5% and 26.5% of CSF variance and was associated with age (−log10 p = 7.1). The other eight factors each explained 0.5-3.7% of RNA-seq variance.

No factor was active in only one sex. Each factor explained a similar share of variance in women and men. In PDBP, the projected factors explained 52.4% of RNA-seq variance in women and 57.7% in men, and 27.1% and 26.3% of CSF variance (CSF in 41 women and 66 men). Factor 1 explained less in PDBP (43.5% and 48.8%), as expected for data the model was not trained on, and Factor 2 explained a similar share of CSF variance (26.9% and 26.3%). The rank order of per-factor RNA-seq R² agreed between cohorts (Spearman ρ = 0.85 in women, 0.78 in men). For CSF the agreement was lower (0.30 and 0.52), because only Factor 2 explained more than 1% of CSF variance and the other factors' near-zero values ranked at random.

Nine of ten factors were stable across subsamples. Factor 6 was reproduced in 8 of 10 subsets, because it partly merged with Factor 5, which explains a similar small share of variance. Factor 2 was reproduced in 9 of 10. The remaining subset failed to reproduce it in both the main and the three-view model. In the three-view model, where we examined that subset, the model captured a weaker CSF pattern that explained half as much CSF variance (10.3-11.4% vs 21.5-26.8%) and did not correlate with Factor 2.

### Factor 7 separated PD from controls in both cohorts

Factor 7 was lower in PD than in controls after adjustment for age and sex (log-odds per SD, PPMI −0.19, q = 0.001; PDBP −0.44, p < 0.001). Its PD effect was similar in women (PPMI −0.16 SD, p = 0.07; PDBP −0.44 SD, p < 0.001) and men (PPMI −0.21 SD, p = 0.004; PDBP −0.36 SD, p < 0.001). Factor 7 explained about 1% of RNA-seq variance, reproduced in all 10 subsets (|r| ≥ 0.88), and was also associated with age (−log10 p = 9.5 in PPMI, 13.1 in PDBP). Factor 7 was not correlated with library size or the number of genes detected per sample (|ρ| ≤ 0.06 in both cohorts), and its PD association was unchanged when both were added to the model (PPMI −0.20, p < 0.001; PDBP −0.44, p < 0.001).

Factor 4 also met the replication rule (PPMI +0.12, q = 0.03; PDBP +0.19, p = 0.002), with similar effects in women and men (interaction PPMI p = 0.82, PDBP p = 0.64), but it is probably technical. Its highest-weighted genes were long, mostly neuronal genes with little expression in blood, such as CSMD2, GRIN2B, GRIN2A, DSCAM, KIF1A and PAX6. Its scores rose with the number of genes detected per sample (Spearman ρ = 0.48 in PPMI, 0.30 in PDBP), which suggests low-level background reads, for example from pre-mRNA or genomic DNA. With library size and genes detected added to the model, its PD association disappeared in PPMI (+0.05, p = 0.43) but not in PDBP (+0.21, p = 0.001). Before PDBP RNA-seq was normalized for library size, Factor 4 explained about 3% of PDBP RNA-seq variance, twice its PPMI share, and its PD association did not replicate. We do not interpret Factor 4 as PD biology.

Factor 8 was lower in PD in PPMI (−0.18, q = 0.002) but not significantly in PDBP (−0.11, p = 0.06). Within men it met the replication rule (PPMI −0.23, q = 0.02; PDBP −0.19, p = 0.03), but its interaction with sex was null in both cohorts (PPMI p = 0.23, PDBP p = 0.19). The difference between the sexes is therefore one of statistical power, not of effect.

Within PPMI PD cases, higher Factor 7 scores went with milder motor signs (−0.12 SD of MDS-UPDRS Part III per SD, q = 0.001) and better cognition (+0.25 SD of MoCA per SD, q = 0.005). In PDBP, neither association held (−0.01 SD, p = 0.82; +0.01 SD, p = 0.87).

Factor 10 was the main age-related factor (−log10 p = 25.4 in PPMI, 28.4 in PDBP). It had no association with PD after adjustment for age.

### Factor 7 contrasts neutrophil and B-cell transcripts

Factor 7 separated two groups of blood cell genes (Figure 5). Its negative weights, which go with PD, were enriched for neutrophil degranulation (Reactome, NES −2.60, FDR < 0.001), inflammatory response (GO, −2.13) and defense response to Gram-negative bacteria (GO, −2.18). The genes driving these sets encode neutrophil granule proteins, such as LTF, CAMP, DEFA4, CEACAM8, MMP8, MMP9, CD177 and S100A8. Its positive weights, which are lower in PD, were enriched for B-cell receptor signaling, Fc-gamma receptor activation (Reactome, NES +3.11) and immunoglobulin production (GO, +2.49). Their leading edges consisted almost entirely of immunoglobulin heavy- and light-chain genes, with IGLL5 and TCL1A among the top-weighted genes, so these sets mark B-cell and plasma-cell transcripts rather than Fc-receptor signaling as such. In all, 156 gene sets had FDR < 0.05 for Factor 7. Lower Factor 7 in PD therefore corresponds to relatively more neutrophil and fewer B-cell transcripts in whole blood. This matches the higher neutrophil gene expression and lower lymphocyte counts reported in PD in the same cohorts (Craig et al., 2021), and suggests that Factor 7 largely reflects blood cell composition.

Factor 10 had little pathway structure: three gene sets had FDR < 0.05 (Hallmark interferon-gamma response, NES +1.63; Reactome olfactory signaling, −1.85; sensory processing of sound, +1.76), and the last two are unlikely to be meaningful in blood. Its highest-weighted genes were more informative. Genes typical of cytotoxic T and NK cells, such as GZMH, ZNF683, B3GAT1 (CD57), TRGV2 and IFNG, had positive weights, which go with older age. LRRN3 and NRCAM, which are expressed in naive T cells, had negative weights. Factor 10 is therefore consistent with a shift from naive towards cytotoxic lymphocytes with age, but GSEA does not test this directly.

The STRING networks of the top-weighted genes showed the same structure (Figure S2). For Factor 7, 18 of the 50 negative-weight genes formed one dense cluster of neutrophil granule proteins (LTF, CAMP, ELANE, AZU1, DEFA4, CEACAM8, MMP8, MMP9, OLFM4, CD177 and others). The 50 negative-weight genes were enriched for neutrophil degranulation against the model-gene background (Reactome, 26 genes, FDR < 10⁻³²). The positive-weight genes formed only small groups (for example TCL1A-TCL1B), and their enrichment was not significant, because most of the top positive genes were immunoglobulin segments absent from STRING. For Factor 10, six positive-weight genes formed one connected group (ZNF683, GZMH, LAG3, IFNG, B3GAT1, VCAM1). Their enrichment returned no pathway terms, only co-mention with publications on cytotoxic and NK-like T cells. Only 12 of Factor 10's 100 genes had a high-confidence partner.

### No factor's PD association differed by sex

None of the 30 interaction tests (10 factors × diagnosis, MDS-UPDRS Part III and MoCA) replicated. The smallest PPMI q-value for an interaction was 0.21. In PDBP, two of the 30 tests had p < 0.05 (Factor 3 × MDS-UPDRS Part III, p = 0.04; Factor 8 × MoCA, p = 0.045), about what chance would give, and neither was supported in PPMI (q = 0.52 and 0.95). The standard error of the PD × sex term was about 0.11 SD in PPMI and 0.12 SD in PDBP, so these tests had about 80% power to detect sex differences of 0.3 SD or more in a factor's PD effect, before multiple-testing correction. Smaller differences cannot be excluded.

### A model without sex grouping gave the same answer

The single-group model converged after 415 iterations. As expected, it formed a factor that separated the sexes: single-group Factor 3 (4.5% of RNA-seq variance) had a mean score of +2.2 in women and −1.9 in men (sex −log10 p > 300) and had no counterpart in the main model (|r| = 0.02).

The PD-related and age-related factors carried over. Main-model Factor 7 matched single-group Factor 8 (|r| = 0.94), and main-model Factor 10 matched single-group Factor 10 (|r| = 0.93); six further factors matched with |r| = 0.99-1.00. Single-group Factor 8 separated PD from controls (PPMI −0.20, q < 0.001; PDBP −0.50, p < 0.001), with a null interaction with sex (PPMI p = 0.42, PDBP p = 0.64). Factors 4 and 8 also carried over with the same results: main-model Factor 4 matched single-group Factor 5 (|r| = 0.99), whose PD association again met the replication rule (PPMI +0.12, q = 0.045; PDBP +0.19, p = 0.002), and main-model Factor 8 matched single-group Factor 7 (|r| = 0.99), which again met the replication rule in men only. No interaction replicated. The only PPMI interaction with q < 0.05 involved the sex factor and MoCA (q = 0.02) and was null in PDBP (p = 0.82). In PDBP, the single-group factors explained 57.5% of RNA-seq variance and 11.7% of CSF variance. All 10 single-group factors were reproduced in all 10 subsamples (minimum |r| 0.88-1.00).

The CSF factor did not carry over. The main model's Factor 2 had no match in the single-group model (best |r| = 0.27). The single-group CSF factor explained 13.5% of CSF variance, CSF R² fell to 14.4%, and that factor was associated with sex (−log10 p = 15.5). The CSF axis is reproducible within one design but depends on the design.

## Discussion

Neither MOFA design found a multi-omic axis of blood or CSF variation whose relation to PD differs by sex. The PD-related factor that replicated and survived adjustment for technical covariates, Factor 7, had similar effects in women and men in both cohorts. This result held when factors were centered within each sex and when average sex differences were kept.

The negative result has a defined scope. MOFA finds the largest axes of shared variation, and in these data those are blood cell composition (Factor 1) and a CSF axis (Factor 2). The PD signal, Factor 7, explains about 1% of RNA-seq variance. A sex-specific PD effect smaller than this axis would not form a factor of its own, and the interaction tests had power only for differences of about 0.3 SD or more. Our results therefore exclude large sex-specific multi-omic PD axes, not sex differences in PD biology in general. Gene-level or protein-level tests of sex × diagnosis interaction are better suited to small effects.

The PD effect of Factor 7 was more than twice as large in PDBP as in PPMI (−0.44 vs −0.19 log-odds per SD), and its associations with motor severity and cognition within PD held in PPMI only. PPMI enrolled people with recently diagnosed PD (Parkinson Progression Marker Initiative, 2011), whereas PDBP includes people with longer disease duration and on dopaminergic treatment. Either could change the blood transcriptome, so part of Factor 7 may reflect disease duration or treatment rather than PD itself. Adjusting for both in PDBP would test this. The enrichment points the same way: Factor 7 mostly tracks the balance of neutrophil and B-cell transcripts. Adjusting for measured blood counts, or for cell proportions estimated from the RNA-seq, would show whether it carries a PD signal beyond cell composition.

Factor 4 shows that meeting the replication rule is not enough for factors this small. It replicated only after PDBP was normalized for library size, its top genes are not expressed in blood, and its PD association in PPMI disappeared after adjustment for genes detected. A technical difference between PD and control samples would replicate across cohorts if both were processed the same way. Factors that explain about 1% of variance should be checked against technical covariates such as library size, genes detected and RNA quality before they are interpreted. The number of genes detected also tracks Factor 1 (ρ = 0.82 in PPMI) and therefore probably blood cell composition, so adjusting for it is conservative.

Several limitations apply. CSF proteomics was available for 354 PPMI and 107 PDBP participants, so CSF results rest on far fewer people than RNA-seq results. The CSF factor depended on model design and failed in one subsample, which suggests that the 5,000 RNA-seq genes dominate the PCA initialization over the 249 CSF proteins; weighting views by feature count or adding factors may stabilize it. Non-detects were treated as missing, but they are missing because the protein was low, which MOFA's missing-data model does not represent. The analysis is cross-sectional at baseline and does not address sex differences in progression. Finally, plasma proteomics did not contribute, so these conclusions apply to blood RNA-seq and CSF proteomics only.
